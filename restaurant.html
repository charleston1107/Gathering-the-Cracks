Previously an old storage room on Bacheng Old Street, the restaurant was refurbished by the restaurant owner as not only a wish to preserve the nostalgic tastes of home cooking when he was small, but also to tell people a fact that heritage does not need governmental certification or legends to exist or to be remembered.

Here, memory is served in bowls: fresh noodles and a broth allowed to thicken, flavours recalled from the two wonton stalls that once anchored the lane. The owner grew up when baskets were lowered from balconies to boats, when flagstones shone from decades of footsteps, and when the teahouse was dim but alive.

His kitchen keeps faith with that ordinary vibrancy. He refuses to costume food in borrowed myths—“I’m frying a pork chop—what story am I suppose to tell?”—insisting that truth tastes better. This room thus becomes a counterexample: heritage as daily practice, sustained by hands, ingredients and neighbours, not plaques, slogans or curated legends.

It invites visitors to taste what the old street once felt like—unvarnished and true
